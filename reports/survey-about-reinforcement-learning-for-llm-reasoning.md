# Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning: A Survey

## TL;DR

- RLVR improves LLM reasoning capabilities [1].
- RLLF enhances logical reasoning in LLMs for legal applications [2].
- Rule-based RL improves reasoning skills in LLMs [3].

## Background

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a critical approach for improving the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs). Early efforts, such as InstructGPT, utilized RLHF to align models with human preferences and improve instruction following [4]. More recently, methods like R$^3$ and Logic-RL have focused on enhancing logical reasoning through RL, leveraging outcome supervision and rule-based frameworks [5][3]. These approaches address the challenge of sparse rewards in outcome supervision by establishing step-wise curricula or emphasizing verification processes [5]. Additionally, research on small LLMs highlights RL's effectiveness in resource-constrained settings, demonstrating significant gains in mathematical reasoning [6]. While RLHF laid the foundation, recent advancements in RLVR and GRPO have enabled more precise and scalable training for reasoning tasks [7].

## Foundational Work

Reinforcement learning (RL) has been pivotal in improving LLM reasoning through various approaches. S-GRPO and T-SPMO enhance model performance under memory constraints [8], while CDAS addresses low sample efficiency by aligning model competence with problem difficulty [9]. GRESO improves training efficiency through selective rollouts [10]. Guru, a cross-domain RL corpus, highlights domain-specific training needs and enhances performance in complex tasks [11]. ARM tackles entropy collapse by dynamic reward shaping [12]. Early foundational work includes InstructGPT, which uses RL from human feedback (RLHF) to align models with instructions, improving truthfulness and helpfulness [4]. These methods collectively address key challenges in RL for LLM reasoning.

## Recent Developments

Recent developments in reinforcement learning (RL) for large language model (LLM) reasoning have focused on improving reasoning accuracy and generalization. Semifactual credit-augmented policy optimization [1] investigates token-level sensitivity to task-irrelevant prompt features, revealing that suppressing high-drift tokens during decoding improves reasoning accuracy without retraining model weights. CARM [13] introduces sequence-level masking to address off-policy mismatches between rollout and training engines, using length-normalized geometric mean of token probability ratios for response contribution. Meanwhile, Logic-RL [3] leverages rule-based RL with synthetic logic puzzles, achieving stable convergence through a system prompt and stringent reward function, resulting in advanced reasoning skills in a 7B model. These methods highlight the importance of reward design and prompt engineering in enhancing LLM reasoning capabilities.

## Evaluation and Limitations

Reinforcement learning (RL) for LLM reasoning faces several evaluation and limitation challenges. First, existing FP4 RL methods suffer from a key limitation: they primarily optimize quantization accuracy on the training and rollout paths independently rather than directly reducing the discrepancy between the two quant, which may limit performance [14]. Second, while RLVR shows promise in improving reasoning capabilities, recent studies question whether it truly incentivizes new reasoning patterns or merely enhances sampling efficiency. Analysis reveals that RLVR-trained models outperform base models at smaller k values but base models achieve higher pass@k scores at larger k [15]. Moreover, the reasoning paths generated by RLVR models are already included in the base models’ sampling distribution, suggesting their reasoning abilities are bounded by the base model [16]. Lastly, a minimalist combination of RL techniques can improve performance over existing strategies, but further exploration is needed to fully leverage the potential of base models [17].

## Trends and open problems

Reinforcement learning (RL) for large language models (LLMs) is gaining traction, with recent studies highlighting its potential to enhance reasoning capabilities in mathematical tasks and code generation [13]. However, challenges such as off-policy responses, computational overhead, and sensitivity to prompt features persist [1]. Researchers are addressing these issues through methods like CARM, which introduces sequence-level masking to handle policy mismatches [13], and TRACE, which focuses on aligning quantization paths during FP4 RL training [14]. Additionally, novel approaches like ARM aim to resolve entropy collapse by dynamically shaping rewards [12]. Despite these advancements, open problems remain, including optimizing reward models for complex reasoning scenarios and reducing sensitivity to irrelevant prompts [18].
